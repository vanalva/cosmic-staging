How to paint kitchen cabinets properly

Cabinets get touched every day, so the prep matters more than the paint. This is the whole job the way we would do it: clean, sand, prime, two thin coats, and enough time to cure.

Before you commit

Is this a DIY for you?

A good project if

  • Your cabinets are solid wood, MDF or already painted, and in sound shape.
  • You can give up the kitchen for a few days.
  • You are patient with prep. It is most of the work, and most of the result.

Think twice if

  • The doors are peeling thermofoil. Paint will not hold on it; the doors usually need replacing.
  • The house was built before 1978 and nobody has tested the old finish for lead.
  • You need the kitchen back tomorrow. Paint needs days to harden.

How the days usually go

  1. Day 1

    Empty, remove, clean, repair, sand and prime (steps 1–8).

  2. Day 2

    Sand the primer, caulk, first coat (steps 9–10).

  3. Day 3

    Light sand and second coat (step 11).

  4. Then

    Let it cure, rehang the doors, go gently for a few weeks (step 12).

Tools and materials

What you’ll need

16 to get

Materials

  • Degreaser

    A kitchen degreaser or TSP substitute, a bucket and non-scratch scrub pads.

  • Bonding primer

    Made for glossy surfaces. Stain-blocking (shellac or oil) on oak or knotty wood.

  • Cabinet paint

    A hard-drying enamel in satin or semi-gloss. About a gallon for an average kitchen.

  • Sanding sponges

    Medium (120–150 grit) to scuff, fine (220) between coats.

  • Wood filler

    For dents and old hardware holes, with a putty knife.

  • Paintable caulk

    For gaps in face frames and along the walls, with a caulk gun.

  • Painter’s tape

    For walls, counters and labelling each door.

  • Drop cloths and plastic

    To cover counters, floors and appliances.

Tools

  • 2-inch angled brush

    A good synthetic brush for edges and profiles.

  • 4-inch fine-finish roller and tray

    Foam or microfiber, for smooth flat panels.

  • Screwdriver or drill

    To take off doors, hinges and pulls.

  • Painter’s pyramids

    Or blocks, so doors dry flat and you can paint both sides.

  • Zip bags and a marker

    One bag of hinges and screws per door.

  • Microfiber or tack cloths

    To lift the sanding dust before each coat.

  • Gloves, glasses and a dust mask

    A respirator if you use oil- or shellac-based primer.

  • Step stool

    For upper cabinets and the tops of the boxes.

How much primer and paint?

Add up the surface of the doors (both sides), drawer fronts, face frames and visible end panels. A typical kitchen comes to roughly 100–200 sq ft.

What are you painting?

Doors: width × height × 2 sides. A 15 by 30 inch door is about 3 sq ft a side.

We take off 20 sq ft per door and 15 sq ft per window.

Coats

Most interior paints cover 250–400 sq ft per gallon. Use the low end on new drywall, texture or bare wood. Check the can.

Preparation

Before you start

Lead paint

If your home was built before 1978, the old finish may contain lead. Test it before you sand (test kits are sold at hardware stores). If it is positive, do not dry-sand: follow lead-safe practices or hire a lead-safe certified contractor.

Air and fumes

Open windows and run a fan blowing out. Wear a dust mask for sanding and a respirator with oil- or shellac-based primer. Keep pilot lights and flames off near solvent products.

Oily rags

Rags soaked in oil-based primer or paint can catch fire by themselves as they dry. Lay them flat outside to dry, or keep them in a closed metal can filled with water until you can dispose of them.

Set up a drying station

Doors dry flat for hours. Clear a spare room or a corner of the garage, lay plastic, and set the doors on painter’s pyramids so you can paint one side, then the other.

Mind the heat and the humidity

Most paints want roughly 50–90°F; check the can. In an Austin summer, paint inside with the AC on rather than in a hot garage: heat dries paint before it levels, and humid days stretch every recoat time.

12 steps

The steps

Tick each step as you finish it. Your progress stays on this device, so you can close the page and pick up where you left off.

  1. 01

    Step 1 of 12

    Empty the cabinets and set up

    About 1 hour

    Take everything out of the cabinets and off the counters. Cover the counters, floor and appliances with drop cloths or plastic.

    Set up the drying station for the doors (see Before you start) before you take anything off.

    Pro tip

    Take a photo of each run of cabinets before you start. It makes putting everything back much quicker.

    Next: step 2
  2. 02

    Step 2 of 12

    Label and remove doors, drawers and hardware

    About 1 hour

    Number each door and drawer front on a piece of tape where the hinge sits, and put the same number inside the cabinet box. The hinge covers the tape, so it survives painting.

    Unscrew the hinges and pulls. Put each door’s hinges and screws in its own labelled bag.

    Common mistake

    Painting around the hinges instead of removing them. The paint cracks the first time the door opens, and the edges look rushed.

    Next: step 3
  3. 03

    Step 3 of 12

    Degrease every surface

    1–2 hours

    Kitchen cabinets carry a film of cooking grease, worst near the stove and around the handles. Paint will not stick to it.

    Scrub every surface you will paint with the degreaser and a non-scratch pad, rinse with clean water, and let it dry completely.

    Safety

    Wear gloves and glasses, and follow the label: some degreasers must be rinsed off or the primer will not bond.

    Next: step 4
  4. 04

    Step 4 of 12

    Fill dents and holes

    30 minutes, plus drying

    Press wood filler into dents, gouges and any hardware holes you will not reuse. Leave it slightly proud of the surface.

    Let it dry for the time on the label. You will sand it flush in the next step.

    Next: step 5
  5. 05

    Step 5 of 12

    Scuff-sand everything

    1–2 hours

    You are dulling the old finish so the primer can grip, not stripping it. Go over every surface with a medium sanding sponge, including the profiles and edges, until the shine is gone evenly.

    Sand the filler flush while you are at it.

    Safety

    Wear a dust mask. In a home built before 1978, test the old finish for lead before you sand (see Before you start).

    Next: step 6
  6. 06

    Step 6 of 12

    Remove all the dust

    20–30 minutes

    Vacuum with a brush attachment, then wipe everything with a damp microfiber cloth or a tack cloth. Dust you leave now becomes grit in the finish.

    Next: step 7
  7. 07

    Step 7 of 12

    Mask the walls, counters and insides

    30–60 minutes

    Tape where the cabinets meet the walls, ceiling, counters and backsplash. Decide whether you are painting the insides of the boxes: most people paint only the face frames and the edges you see with the doors open, and tape off the rest.

    Next: step 8
  8. 08

    Step 8 of 12

    Prime

    1–2 hours, plus drying

    Brush the edges and profiles, then roll one thin, even coat of primer on the boxes, the door backs and the drawer fronts. Turn the doors when the backs are dry to the touch and prime the fronts.

    Let it dry for the recoat time on the can (see Drying and recoat times).

    Pro tip

    Oak grain shows through paint. For a smooth, modern look, fill the grain first with a grain filler or a high-build primer, sanding between coats.

    Next: step 9
  9. 09

    Step 9 of 12

    Sand the primer and caulk the gaps

    About 1 hour

    Sand the primer lightly with fine (220) paper to knock down raised grain and any drips, then remove the dust.

    Run a thin bead of paintable caulk into gaps in the face frames and where the boxes meet the walls. Smooth it with a damp finger and let it dry as the tube says.

    Next: step 10
  10. 10

    Step 10 of 12

    First coat of paint

    2–3 hours

    Brush the recessed edges and profiles first, then roll the flat panels with the fine-finish roller. Finish one whole panel before moving to the next so the edges stay wet.

    Keep it thin. Two thin coats look better and harden faster than one thick one. Do the door backs first; turn the doors when the backs are dry to the touch.

    Watch out

    Check each door for drips along the bottom edge and in the corners for ten minutes after painting it. Brush them out while the paint is still wet.

    Next: step 11
  11. 11

    Step 11 of 12

    Sand lightly, then the second coat

    2–3 hours

    Wait for the full recoat time on the can. Many hybrid cabinet enamels need 16 hours or more, longer in a cool or humid room.

    Sand very lightly with 220–320 grit, remove the dust, and apply the second coat the same way as the first.

    Pro tip

    Work with a light held low, so it rakes across the surface. It shows brush marks and thin spots you would miss under ceiling lights.

    Next: step 12
  12. 12

    Step 12 of 12

    Let it cure, then put it back together

    1–2 hours, after curing

    Dry is not cured. The paint feels dry within hours but stays soft for days. Rehang the doors when they no longer feel tacky and do not take a fingernail print, often two to three days; check the can.

    Put clear bumper pads on the doors and drawers, reinstall the hardware from the labelled bags, and treat the cabinets gently while the paint reaches full hardness, which takes one to four weeks depending on the product.

    Common mistake

    Rehanging too soon. Soft paint sticks to the frame and tears off when the door opens.

What usually goes wrong

Common mistakes

Skipping the degreaser

The paint looks fine for a week, then peels around the handles, exactly where the grease was.

No sanding, no bonding primer

Paint over a glossy finish has nothing to grip. It chips at the first knock.

Thick coats

They drip, sag in the corners and take far longer to harden. Two thin coats beat one heavy one.

Rushing the recoat

A second coat over soft paint drags and wrinkles it. Wait the full time on the can.

Rehanging too soon

Soft paint sticks to the frame and tears when the door opens.

No labels on the doors

Doors that almost fit do not close evenly. Number every door and its hinge spot.

From the crew

Pro tips

Backs first

Paint the backs of the doors first, so the side everyone sees is done last, with the most practice behind it.

Test one door

Test your primer and paint on the back of one door before you do the whole kitchen.

Decant

Pour what you need into a small container and close the can. Skin and dust stay out of the rest.

Touch-up jar

Keep a labelled jar of leftover paint for touch-ups. Note the product and color on the lid.

Bumper pads

Clear bumper pads on every door and drawer protect the new finish more than anything else.
